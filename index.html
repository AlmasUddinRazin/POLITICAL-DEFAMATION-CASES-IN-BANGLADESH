POLITICAL DEFAMATION CASES IN BANGLADESH

Research Question: When does defamation law legitimately protect a person's reputation, and when can its use in political disputes create pressure on critics, journalists or political opponents and restrict freedom of expression?

1. Introduction

Defamation law serves as an essential legal mechanism designed to protect human dignity, personal honor, and social standing from false, harmful attacks. In modern democratic societies, an individual's reputation is recognized as an invaluable personal asset. Once damaged by malicious or reckless falsehoods, it can cause severe emotional distress, social exclusion, and professional harm. Conversely, freedom of expression and open public debate are the cornerstones of democratic governance. The tension between protecting individual reputation and safeguarding the right of citizens to speak freely forms one of the most contentious debates in contemporary jurisprudence.

In Bangladesh, this tension becomes particularly visible in political life. When public officials, political leaders, and party activists become involved in public disputes, legal complaints alleging defamation frequently follow. Defamation proceedings in Bangladesh are predominantly initiated through the criminal justice system rather than civil actions for tortious damages. When legal mechanisms are invoked in politically sensitive environments, a crucial constitutional concern emerges: does the law genuinely serve to vindicate hurt feelings, or is it utilized to suppress legitimate opposition, public criticism, and investigative journalism?

This assignment addresses a central research question: when does defamation law legitimately protect a person's reputation, and when can its use in political disputes create undue pressure on critics, journalists, or political opponents and restrict freedom of expression? As the foundational component of this collaborative legal inquiry, this section establishes the conceptual and statutory bedrock of the research. It explores the nature and tortious origins of defamation, examines the primary statutory provisions under Bangladeshi criminal law, analyses constitutional boundaries under Article 39, examines procedural limitations under the Code of Criminal Procedure 1898, and defines the social and institutional dynamics of political defamation in Bangladesh.

2. Legal Framework of Defamation in Bangladesh

2.1 Definition and Essential Elements of Defamation

At its legal core, defamation is the wrongful publication of a false statement that damages the reputation of another person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society. In general law and classical tort theory, actionable defamation requires three fundamental ingredients. The first is defamatory meaning: the statement must tend to lower the plaintiff in the estimation of right-thinking members of society, expose them to hatred, contempt, or ridicule, or cause them to be shunned or avoided. The second is reference to the complainant: the words must reasonably be understood to refer directly to the person claiming the injury. The third is publication: the statement must be communicated or published to at least one third party other than the person defamed.

In common law jurisdictions, defamation is divided into libel (defamation in permanent form, such as written text, printed articles, or broadcast media) and slander (defamation in non-permanent, spoken, or gestural form). Historically, the primary remedy for tortious defamation has been civil damages aimed at compensating the injured party for actual reputational loss.

2.2 Criminal Defamation under the Penal Code 1860

Unlike common law jurisdictions that rely primarily on civil litigation in tort, Bangladeshi legal practice relies overwhelmingly on criminal provisions. Defamation is explicitly codified in Section 499 of the Penal Code 1860. Under Section 499, defamation is defined as making or publishing any imputation concerning any person by words (either spoken or intended to be read), signs, or visible representations, intending to harm, or knowing or having reason to believe that such imputation will harm, the reputation of such person. Section 500 of the Penal Code prescribes the punishment, stating that whoever defames another may be punished with simple imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years, or with fine, or with both.

2.3 Statutory Exceptions to Section 499

To prevent the law from stifling legitimate speech, Section 499 provides ten specific statutory exceptions where an imputation does not amount to criminal defamation. The first is the imputation of truth for the public good: it is not defamation to impute anything which is true concerning any person, if it is for the public good that the imputation should be made or published. The second concerns the public conduct of public servants, namely expressing in good faith any opinion respecting the conduct of a public servant in the discharge of public functions. The third concerns the conduct of any person touching any public question, namely expressing in good faith any opinion respecting that conduct. The fourth permits the publication of substantially true reports of the proceedings of courts. The fifth permits expressing in good faith any opinion respecting the merits of any case decided by a court, or the conduct of witnesses. The sixth permits expressing in good faith any opinion respecting the merits of any performance submitted to the judgment of the public. The seventh protects censure passed in good faith by a person having lawful authority over another. The eighth protects an accusation preferred in good faith against any person to anyone who has lawful authority over that person. The ninth protects an imputation made in good faith on the character of another for the protection of the interests of the maker or any other person, or for the public good. The tenth protects a caution conveyed in good faith to one person against another, for the good of that person or for the public good.

In political disputes, Exceptions 1, 2, 3, and 9 are critical. However, under criminal procedural rules, the burden of establishing that an imputation falls within these exceptions typically rests on the accused person during trial, creating substantial pre-trial procedural hardship.

2.4 Constitutional Guarantees: Article 39

Statutory provisions cannot be viewed in isolation from constitutional mandates. Article 39 of the Constitution of Bangladesh protects fundamental rights related to expression. Article 39(1) guarantees freedom of thought and conscience. Article 39(2) guarantees, subject to any reasonable restrictions imposed by law in the interests of the security of the State, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, or in relation to contempt of court, defamation or incitement to an offence, the right of every citizen to freedom of speech and expression, and freedom of the press.

Article 39 explicitly identifies "defamation" as a permissible ground for imposing restrictions on speech. However, modern constitutional interpretation demands that any restriction must be "reasonable" and proportionate. A statutory restriction that silences fair political commentary or shields public officials from public accountability violates the democratic intent of the Constitution.

2.5 Procedural Standing: Section 198 of the Code of Criminal Procedure 1898

Procedural safeguards are vital to prevent vexatious litigation. Under Section 198 of the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC) 1898, a criminal court is barred from taking cognizance of an offence under Chapter XXI of the Penal Code (which includes Sections 499 and 500) except upon a formal complaint made by "some person aggrieved by the offence". The fundamental purpose of Section 198 is to ensure that only the individual whose personal reputation has actually suffered direct injury may initiate criminal proceedings. Third parties, distant party loyalists, or admirers cannot legally claim to be an "aggrieved person" merely because their political leader was criticized. Strict judicial adherence to Section 198 acts as an indispensable filter against proxy litigation.

3. Political Defamation in Bangladesh

3.1 Meaning and Characteristics

Political defamation refers to the invocation of defamation laws, overwhelmingly criminal complaints, in the context of party politics, elections, governance critiques, policy disagreements, and public advocacy. It carries distinctive legal and structural characteristics. First, there are asymmetrical power dynamics: public officeholders and influential politicians wield state machinery, extensive financial resources, and dedicated party support that ordinary citizens and independent journalists lack. Second, there is the weaponization of legal process: the process itself often becomes the punishment, as criminal summonses, arrest warrants, and repeated court appearances in distant geographical jurisdictions impose heavy financial and psychological burdens on defendants before any trial on the merits occurs. Third, there are proxy and multi-district filings: party supporters frequently file simultaneous criminal complaints across multiple district courts for a single spoken remark or published news report, exploiting Section 500 to overwhelm the respondent.

3.2 Public Figures versus Private Citizens

In established common law jurisprudence, public figures, including politicians, ministers, and high-ranking public servants, are subject to a higher threshold of public scrutiny than private citizens. By actively seeking public office and exercising governmental authority, public figures voluntarily expose their official conduct and personal fitness to intense public debate. When public figures treat political criticism or investigative reporting as personal defamation, legitimate political accountability is compromised. Instead of utilizing civil rebuttals or relying on tortious compensation, resorting to criminal defamation creates a chilling environment where journalists, civil society members, and ordinary citizens are forced into self-censorship to avoid criminal liability.

Establishing this legal and procedural baseline clarifies that defamation law in Bangladesh requires careful re-examination to reconcile individual reputation with constitutional freedoms.

4. Misuse of Defamation Laws in Bangladesh

Defamation laws are intended to protect a person's reputation and dignity from false and harmful statements. However, these laws may create problems when they are used to discourage criticism or put pressure on people who express opinions about public affairs. In Bangladesh, this issue is particularly important because political disagreements, criticism of public officials and media reporting often involve sensitive subjects. Political leaders and public figures have the right to protect their reputations, but citizens and journalists also have the right to question their activities and decisions. The main concern is not the existence of defamation law itself, but the possibility of using it beyond its proper purpose.

One major concern in Bangladesh is the use of legal threats or complaints against political critics and journalists. Political leaders and public officials regularly become subjects of newspaper reports, television discussions, online articles and social media criticism. Some of these statements may be unfair or false, and genuine defamation should have a legal remedy. However, when a legal threat is made in response to satire, criticism or reporting on a matter of public interest, it may create fear among journalists and citizens. People may begin to believe that criticising an influential person could lead to legal trouble, even when their intention is simply to raise a public concern.

A relevant example is the incident involving Dhaka South City Corporation Mayor Sheikh Fazle Noor Taposh and The Daily Star in June 2023. The newspaper had published a satirical piece titled "Cutting trees to make way for air" in its weekly Satireday section. Taposh's lawyer issued a legal notice demanding Tk 100 crore in compensation and requesting the removal of the article and an unconditional apology. The newspaper explained that the article was satire and was intended to draw attention to a public-interest issue. It subsequently removed the article from its website after receiving the notice. This incident raised questions about the limits of political satire and the pressure that a large compensation demand can place on a media organisation. It is important to note that this was a legal notice, not a court judgment finding the newspaper guilty of defamation.

Another concern is the burden of facing legal proceedings. A person accused of defamation may have to spend money on lawyers, attend court hearings and take time away from work or education. These difficulties can be particularly serious for local journalists, independent reporters and ordinary citizens who have limited financial resources. Even if a person believes that their statement was true or made in good faith, the possibility of a lengthy legal process may discourage them from speaking further. In this way, the pressure of legal action can affect freedom of expression even before a court reaches a final decision.

The experience of Bangladeshi journalists provides another important example. In December 2017, the Committee to Protect Journalists (CPJ) published a report discussing how defamation and other speech-related laws could be used to harass journalists. It referred to journalist Abdul Latif Morol, who faced arrest under section 57 of the Information and Communication Technology Act after posting on Facebook about a goat donated by a state minister that reportedly died shortly afterwards. Although this was not a prosecution under sections 499–500 of the Penal Code, the incident showed how laws dealing with online expression could also create pressure on journalists. It also demonstrated that legal action over a social media post could have serious consequences for an individual.

The issue has continued to appear in more recent reporting. In February 2025, CPJ reported that four Bangladeshi journalists were facing possible criminal defamation proceedings following a complaint filed in November 2024 by a businesswoman. The complaint concerned their reporting on alleged business practices and labour violations involving two companies. The journalists included editors and reporters from different Bangladeshi media outlets. CPJ reported that the police had been directed to investigate the complaint. This example is important because it shows that defamation proceedings may also arise from investigative reporting about private businesses, not only from political disputes. However, the allegations in the complaint and the journalists' legal responsibility must be determined through the proper judicial process.

There is also a concern about unequal access to legal resources. Influential individuals and organisations may have greater financial ability to pursue legal action, while the person facing the complaint may struggle to defend themselves. This difference can create pressure on smaller newspapers, independent journalists and ordinary citizens. A person may decide not to publish a report or express an opinion simply because they cannot afford the possible consequences. As a result, the law may discourage public discussion even when the statement concerns an issue that deserves attention.

Therefore, misuse should not be understood as every complaint filed by a public figure. A person who has genuinely suffered reputational harm must be able to seek justice. The problem arises when legal proceedings are used to intimidate critics, discourage responsible reporting or avoid public accountability. Each complaint should be examined according to its facts, evidence, intention and the protections available under the law.

5. Challenges to Freedom of Expression in Bangladesh

Freedom of expression is an important part of democracy because it allows people to share opinions, question public decisions and take part in national discussions. Article 39 of the Constitution of Bangladesh guarantees freedom of thought and conscience, freedom of speech and expression, and freedom of the press, subject to certain reasonable restrictions imposed by law. Defamation is one of the matters recognised as a ground for restriction. Therefore, freedom of expression in Bangladesh is not unlimited. However, restrictions should be applied carefully so that the protection of reputation does not prevent fair criticism or responsible journalism.

One of the biggest challenges is self-censorship. Self-censorship means that a person chooses not to express an opinion or publish information because of fear of legal action, pressure or other consequences. In Bangladesh, a journalist may hesitate to publish an investigative report about alleged corruption or irregularities involving a powerful person. Similarly, a student or ordinary citizen may avoid posting a political opinion on Facebook because they are worried that their words could lead to a complaint. This fear can reduce public discussion and prevent important information from reaching citizens.

Journalists face particular difficulties because their work often involves examining the activities of political leaders, government officials and powerful organisations. Reporting on public spending, corruption allegations, election-related matters and government decisions may create disagreement with the people concerned. However, such reporting is an important part of accountability. If journalists are unable to investigate and report on matters of public interest, citizens may not receive the information they need to judge public institutions and their representatives.

The experience of Prothom Alo in March 2023 demonstrates the wider challenges faced by the Bangladeshi media. Journalist Shamsuzzaman Shams was arrested in connection with a report about the rising cost of living, and a case was filed under the Digital Security Act. The case was not a criminal defamation prosecution under sections 499–500 of the Penal Code. Nevertheless, the incident attracted serious concern about the treatment of journalists reporting on public issues. Human Rights Watch's World Report 2024 also discussed pressure on journalists and newsrooms, including demands to remove articles and the growth of self-censorship. This shows that freedom of expression can be affected by different laws and forms of pressure, not only by defamation proceedings.

Social media has created both opportunities and challenges for freedom of expression in Bangladesh. Platforms such as Facebook and YouTube allow people to discuss political events, criticise public decisions and share information without depending entirely on traditional media. Students, activists and ordinary citizens can now participate in national discussions more easily. However, false information, edited photographs, misleading statements and personal attacks can also spread quickly. Such content can seriously damage a person's reputation. Therefore, the law must protect people against genuinely harmful false statements while recognising the importance of fair comment, criticism and public-interest discussion.

A further challenge is the difficulty of distinguishing between criticism of public conduct and an attack on personal reputation. For example, saying that a government policy has failed is generally a political opinion. On the other hand, making an unsupported allegation that a particular public official has committed a crime may seriously harm that person's reputation. Similarly, a journalist who reports a matter after reasonable verification should not automatically be treated in the same way as someone who deliberately spreads false information. The context, evidence and purpose of a statement are important when deciding whether it amounts to defamation.

The exceptions under section 499 of the Penal Code are therefore significant. They recognise certain situations in which statements may be protected, including statements made in good faith concerning public servants and questions of public interest, subject to the conditions of the law. However, people who are not familiar with these legal protections may still be afraid of expressing their opinions. Journalists and ordinary citizens may not know whether a particular statement will be protected if a complaint is filed against them. This uncertainty can make people more cautious when discussing political and social issues.

6. Case Studies and Comparative Analysis

This part uses the IRAC method (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) to examine two Bangladeshi examples and one international judgment, distinguishing allegations from findings.

6.1 Case One: Gazi Tariq Salman Defamation Case (2017), Bangladesh

Facts and background. Gazi Tariq Salman was an Upazila Nirbahi Officer (UNO). In 2017, invitation cards for an Independence Day programme in Agailjhara, Barisal, used a portrait of Bangabandhu Sheikh Mujibur Rahman drawn by a child in an art competition. Obaidullah Saju, a local Awami League leader and president of the Barisal District Lawyers' Association, objected, alleging that the portrait was distorted and dishonouring. On 7 June 2017, Saju filed a Tk 5 crore complaint reportedly under Penal Code section 501, which concerns printing or engraving defamatory matter with knowledge or reason to believe it is defamatory. Salman was sent to custody when he appeared in court in July and later obtained bail. On 23 July, Saju sought withdrawal and the court allowed it. No full trial determined guilt.

Issue. Was the prosecution justified, and was the complainant entitled to sue over an alleged insult to another person's image?

Rule. Penal Code sections 499–500 define and punish criminal defamation, and section 501 concerns knowingly or recklessly printing or engraving defamatory matter. CrPC section 198 generally requires a complaint by a person aggrieved.

Application. It was unclear from reports whether Saju's own reputation was harmed. Offence at an image is not automatically reputational injury. The child's authorship also raised a question about Salman's knowledge. Withdrawal meant the court did not decide these issues at trial.

Conclusion. The complaint was withdrawn in July 2017 and Salman was not convicted after trial. The case illustrates the need to examine complainant standing, the precise offence and evidence of the required mental element before criminal proceedings continue.

Critical analysis. A public officer faced custody before the complaint was withdrawn. No court finding that the case was malicious should be claimed. The lesson is that defamation protects reputation, not every feeling of offence, and courts must test whose reputation was harmed.

6.2 Case Two: Khaleda Zia's Five Defamation Cases (2014–2017; dismissed in 2024), Bangladesh

Facts and background. Former Prime Minister and BNP chairperson Khaleda Zia faced five defamation proceedings filed between 2014 and 2017. They concerned alleged statements or conduct about the Liberation War, Bangabandhu, the national flag and map, and her birthday celebration on 15 August. Several complaints were filed by AB Siddique of Bangladesh Jananetri Parishad, while another birthday case was filed by Gazi Zahirul Islam. The allegations included remarks said to hurt religious feelings or demean Liberation War martyrs, a statement about Bangabandhu's role before independence, and the 15 August birthday celebration. These were not judicial findings. On 3 September 2024, three Dhaka courts dismissed the five proceedings after complainants repeatedly failed to appear or take necessary steps. Reports said Siddique had died several years earlier. No full trial decided whether each statement was defamatory.

Issue. How should courts handle political defamation complaints that complainants do not pursue?

Rule. Penal Code sections 499–500 govern criminal defamation and its exceptions, including qualified protection for good-faith statements on public conduct or questions. CrPC section 198 regulates complainants. Article 39 of the Constitution protects expression subject to restrictions including defamation.

Application. Political sensitivity proves neither misuse nor defamation. Courts should examine the words, context, publication, affected person and exceptions. The reported dismissal followed complainants' failure to take steps; it was procedural, not a detailed decision on the truth or legality of the statements.

Conclusion. The five proceedings were dismissed in September 2024 because the complainants did not pursue them. They demonstrate the need for timely case management and an accurate distinction between allegations and judicial findings.

Critical analysis. Long-pending cases burden accused persons, and non-appearance wastes court time. Courts should manage dormant cases fairly. Politicians retain reputational rights, and complaints should be assessed neutrally.

6.3 Case Three: New York Times Co. v Sullivan (1964), United States

Facts and background. In 1960, The New York Times published an advertisement supporting civil-rights activists and criticising police treatment of protestors in Montgomery, Alabama. Some details were inaccurate. City commissioner L. B. Sullivan, whose duties involved the police department, argued that the criticism reflected on him. Though not named, he sued. An Alabama jury awarded him US$500,000, and state courts upheld it. The newspaper appealed to the US Supreme Court.

Issue. Must a public official prove knowing or reckless falsity to win a claim about official conduct?

Rule. Under the First Amendment, a public official claiming defamation over official conduct must prove actual malice, meaning knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth. It does not simply mean hatred.

Application. The advertisement contained errors, but evidence did not show that the newspaper knew they were false or recklessly disregarded the truth. Inaccuracy alone could not sustain the award.

Conclusion. On 9 March 1964, the Supreme Court reversed the judgment. It established the actual-malice standard for public officials claiming defamation over official conduct, which became a major protection for public debate in the United States.

Critical analysis and relevance to Bangladesh. Sullivan protects criticism of officials from liability for mere errors, while retaining a remedy for knowing or reckless falsehoods. It is not binding in Bangladesh but offers a useful comparative model.

6.4 Comparative Analysis

Tariq Salman involved a public officer and a child's drawing; Khaleda Zia's proceedings involved political statements and absent complainants; Sullivan produced a final constitutional judgment. Withdrawal and non-prosecution dismissal do not establish truth or absence of defamation. Sullivan decided a legal standard and reversed damages. Together, the cases show the importance of standing and of balancing reputation with public debate. The Bangladeshi case details rely on news reports, as the original complaints and full court orders were unavailable.

7. Recommendations for Legal Reform in Bangladesh

7.1 Review and Reform of Sections 499–500 of the Penal Code 1860

The government should consider reviewing Sections 499 and 500 to ensure that criminal defamation provisions are not unnecessarily applied to legitimate political criticism, satire, journalism and public-interest discussions. Although Section 499 contains ten exceptions, including statements concerning public servants and matters of public interest, their practical application should be strengthened. In particular, good-faith opinions and responsible reporting about public officials should receive meaningful legal protection. Criminal punishment should be reserved for genuinely serious cases involving intentional or knowingly false statements that cause substantial reputational harm. This would help maintain the original purpose of defamation law while reducing the possibility of its misuse.

7.2 Consider Decriminalisation and Strengthening Civil Remedies

Bangladesh should seriously consider whether criminal defamation should be decriminalised or, at minimum, more narrowly defined. Criminal punishment, including imprisonment of up to two years under Section 500, may create disproportionate pressure in disputes involving political opinions or public criticism. Civil remedies could provide a more balanced alternative by allowing injured individuals to seek reasonable compensation for actual reputational damage. Other remedies, such as correction, retraction and a public apology, may also be appropriate depending on the circumstances. However, criminal liability should not automatically arise from every inaccurate statement or disagreement. The objective should be to protect reputation through effective remedies without creating unnecessary fear of imprisonment.

7.3 Stronger Protection for Freedom of Expression and Journalism

Article 39 of the Constitution of Bangladesh guarantees freedom of speech and expression and freedom of the press, subject to reasonable restrictions, including those relating to defamation. Therefore, courts should carefully balance reputational protection against the public's right to receive information and participate in political discussions. Journalists should not face legal pressure merely for reporting on corruption allegations, government decisions or matters of public concern, provided they act responsibly and in good faith. The incidents involving The Daily Star and other journalists discussed in this assignment demonstrate why stronger protection for responsible journalism is necessary. At the same time, deliberate misinformation and knowingly harmful false allegations should remain subject to appropriate legal remedies.

7.4 Strict Judicial Examination of Complainant Standing under Section 198 CrPC

Courts should strictly apply Section 198 of the Code of Criminal Procedure 1898, which requires a complaint by a person aggrieved by the alleged offence. A person should not be permitted to initiate criminal defamation proceedings merely because they support a political leader or feel offended by criticism directed towards another individual. The Tariq Salman case illustrates the importance of examining whether the complainant's own reputation was directly affected. Before proceeding, courts should identify the person allegedly defamed and determine whether the complainant has the necessary legal standing. This safeguard would help prevent unnecessary proxy complaints and protect individuals from proceedings initiated without a genuine personal grievance.

7.5 Prevention of Repetitive and Abusive Legal Proceedings

The judiciary should introduce stronger case-management safeguards to prevent repetitive complaints, unnecessary delays and proceedings that are not properly pursued. Courts should examine the alleged defamatory words, publication, available evidence, applicable exceptions and the required mental element at an early stage. Complaints involving substantially the same statement and parties should receive appropriate judicial scrutiny to prevent unnecessary duplication. The dismissal of Khaleda Zia's five defamation proceedings in September 2024, following the complainants' failure to pursue them, highlights the importance of timely judicial case management. Courts should ensure that dormant proceedings are dealt with efficiently while preserving the accused person's right to a fair hearing.

7.6 Protection of Journalists and Whistleblowers

Bangladesh should develop practical safeguards for journalists, researchers, activists and whistleblowers who disclose information in good faith on matters of public interest. Such protection should include access to legal assistance, reasonable procedural safeguards and recognition of responsible investigative reporting. A distinction must be maintained between genuine investigative journalism and the deliberate publication of fabricated allegations. The comparative decision in New York Times Co v Sullivan offers a useful model: it established that a public official claiming defamation concerning official conduct must prove actual malice, meaning knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth. Although this American judgment is not binding in Bangladesh, its reasoning may guide future legal reform. Any adoption should be carefully adapted to Bangladesh's constitutional and statutory framework.

8. Conclusion

Defamation law plays an important role in protecting personal dignity, reputation and social standing. However, its application must not undermine the democratic values of freedom of expression, political accountability and press freedom. The research demonstrates that Bangladesh faces the challenge of maintaining this balance.

The Tariq Salman case highlights the importance of complainant standing and proper judicial scrutiny before criminal proceedings create serious personal consequences. The dismissal of Khaleda Zia's five defamation proceedings demonstrates the need for effective case management and timely disposal of complaints. Meanwhile, New York Times Co v Sullivan provides a comparative example of stronger protection for criticism of public officials while retaining remedies for knowingly or recklessly false statements.

Ultimately, Bangladesh should not eliminate the legitimate protection provided by defamation law. Instead, it should reform its application through stronger procedural safeguards, meaningful protection for good-faith public-interest expression and appropriate civil remedies. A balanced legal framework would protect individuals against genuine reputational injury while ensuring that journalists, citizens and political critics can express their views without unnecessary fear of criminal proceedings. The ultimate objective of defamation law should be to protect reputation without silencing legitimate expression, because both are essential to a fair and democratic society.
